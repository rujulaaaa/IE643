1. Project snapshot

Team: Claudn't · Roll numbers: [fill in, one per member]

The task. A video diffusion U-Net does two jobs in alternation. Its spatial layers work inside a single frame and decide what the thing looks like; its temporal layers work across frames and decide how it moves. Our job is to find out where that division actually sits inside the network, which layers and which denoising timesteps, and then prove we found it by making an edit that only that understanding would allow. The target demonstration is a clip of a golden retriever running on a beach where we keep the exact running gait but turn the dog into a horse, and the reverse, where the dog stays but starts walking instead.

Why this is a probing project and not a generation project. We are not trying to build a better video model. We are trying to explain one. The edit at the end is evidence, not the product. That framing matters for how we spend compute: a careful sweep over a small, controlled clip set beats a large, messy one.

What changed in this revision. The previous draft left roughly twenty methodological choices implicit — the distance function behind the central figure, how pairs are drawn, how many seeds, what counts as success, and what the "trained model" deliverable actually is. Every one of those is now fixed in writing. Section 5.7 is the register; it is the section to read if you are reviewing this document against the earlier one.

Timeline. Course staff extended the overall project by roughly a month and a half, which puts the intensive assessment somewhere in early-to-mid November 2026. We have not seen a firm date yet, so everything past 1 November in the table in section 6 is provisional. The prep presentation deadline (21 September) may or may not have moved with it; we are treating it as still live and submitting on time.

2.3 What we decided because of the pilots

  • AnimateDiff is the primary model. E1 settled the feasibility question and E4 confirmed the motion module is a real causal handle, not just a label on a box.
  • Every clip in the probing set is generated from a fixed seed and a fixed noise tensor. E2 is the justification. Without it, activation differences between two clips are dominated by sampling noise and the whole probe is meaningless. How many seeds, and how they are assigned, is now fixed in D-04.
  • The harness stores per-coordinate summary statistics by default. E3 killed the original plan of dumping every activation to disk, at 3.4 GB per clip.
  • Content pairs are drawn within a body-plan family. E2's noun-swap failure is a direct threat to the controlled design, so the subject vocabulary is split into two families and cross-family pairs are reported separately as a labelled hard subset. See D-05.
  • The trained-model deliverable gets a feasibility pilot of its own. E5, above.

4.1 The probing set, fully specified

We generate our own clips. That is not a shortcut; it is the only way to get pairs that differ along exactly one axis, and without that the probe has nothing to measure. The previous draft gave the shape of this set but not its contents. Both are now fixed, because a sweep run against an unwritten vocabulary is not reproducible.

The frozen vocabulary

15 subjects × 4 verbs × 2 scenes = 120 clips per seed. The prompt template is "a {subject} {verb} {scene}, high quality, detailed" with the negative prompt "bad quality, worse quality, blurry" held constant. Subjects are split into two body-plan families so that content pairs can be drawn within a family (D-05).

All four verbs are plausible for all fifteen subjects, which is why this particular list was chosen: a verb that only applies to some subjects would silently turn a motion pair into a content pair.

How a pair is built

A pair is two clips that share a seed, a noise tensor, a sampler and every prompt token but one. The anchor design makes the count deterministic rather than "whichever pairs we happened to use".

  • Content pairs. Hold verb and scene, swap the subject against the family anchor. Within family Q: 7 partners × 4 verbs × 2 scenes = 56. Within family H the anchor is young man: 6 × 4 × 2 = 48. Total 104 within-family content pairs.
  • Motion pairs. Hold subject and scene, swap the verb against the anchor running: 3 partners × 15 subjects × 2 scenes = 90 motion pairs.
  • Cross-family content pairs. A separate labelled set of 56 pairs (quadruped against human, same verb and scene). These are the hard subset E2 warned us about, reported separately and never pooled into the headline figure.

Seeds

The whole grid is regenerated under three seeds — 42, 1337, 2024 — giving 360 clips. A pair always lives inside one seed. The selectivity score is computed within a seed and then averaged across seeds, and the spread across seeds is the error bar on the central figure. One seed would leave us unable to say whether the map is a property of the model or of one noise tensor, which is the first question any examiner will ask. At 94 s per clip this costs 9.4 GPU-hours, which section 7 shows we can afford.

Where the vocabulary comes from

  • Subjects and scenes sampled from MSR-VTT and WebVid-10M captions, so we are not testing on a handful of toy prompts. WebVid in particular is close to what these models were pretrained on.
  • Verbs taken from UCF101 and Kinetics-400 action labels, which give a clean standard list of distinct motions instead of whatever we happened to think of.
Data provenance, stated precisely. For the probing set of Deliverables 1 to 4 we train on none of these datasets; they are a source of vocabulary and structure only, and every clip we probe is generated by the model itself. Section 5.5 is the one exception and we flag it rather than let it be discovered: the LoRA pair is fitted on roughly 20 real clips drawn from UCF101 and Kinetics-400. That is a deliberate, bounded use of real video for the trained-model deliverable, it is disclosed in the report's data section, and it touches nothing in Deliverables 1 to 4.

Pretraining data of the models we use

Required by the presentation checklist and worth stating plainly. The SD1.5 backbone was trained on LAION-5B subsets (image–text pairs, web-scraped, known to carry demographic and aesthetic bias). The AnimateDiff motion module was trained on WebVid-10M (short stock-footage clips, watermarked, biased towards professionally shot content and slow camera motion). Both biases are visible in our own pilots — the beach scenes look like stock footage — and they bound what our conclusions can claim: we are characterising this model, not video diffusion in general.

5. Refined plan, deliverable by deliverable

This section is where the reading in group 5 changed things. The original plan was to sweep every layer and every timestep and look for structure. That is still the shape of it, but we now know enough to predict what we should find, which makes the result falsifiable instead of merely descriptive. There are now five deliverables: the trained model in 5.5 has been promoted from an afterthought to a numbered workstream, because it is a graded item and because, done the way described there, it tests Deliverable 2 rather than merely sitting beside it.

5.1 Deliverable 1: setup and the extraction harness

  • Hook targets. In diffusers, AnimateDiff builds a UNetMotionModel from a UNet2DConditionModel plus a MotionAdapter. The temporal path is the motion_modules list inside each down_blocks[i], up_blocks[i] and the mid block — 21 modules in total (8 down, 1 mid, 12 up). The spatial path is the transformer_blocks, with attn1 self-attention and attn2 cross-attention to the text embedding. Forward hooks on both, keyed by module path.
  • Coordinate system. Everything is indexed by a triple: (module path, denoising timestep, clip id). Every later stage queries by that triple, so getting it right once saves a lot of pain.
  • The two-tier sweep. The coarse pass takes one representative module per block — 9 temporal coordinates and 7 spatial — at all 25 timesteps. The refined pass expands only inside whichever block wins, at full module resolution. This is what makes the sweep affordable; see D-07 and D-08.
  • What gets stored. Not the raw tensors, by default: E3 measured a full dump at 3.4 GB per clip. Instead we store, per coordinate, the channel means and variances, the norms of the frame-mean-removed residual, and cross-attention maps head-averaged and downsampled to a 16 × 16 spatial grid with the frame axis kept, for the subject, verb and scene tokens only. Full tensors are retained for a short list of coordinates we mark as interesting (D-09).
  • Determinism. One seed policy, written down and enforced in the generation script rather than passed by hand. Every clip records the seed, sampler, step count, resolution, base-model repository id and commit hash alongside its activations.
  • Attention maps. DAAM-style aggregation, extended to video by keeping the frame axis rather than collapsing it. A token's map becomes a small movie, which is what makes it useful for telling content attention apart from motion attention.

5.2 Deliverable 2: three predictions, then a measurement

Rather than sweeping blind, we go in with three hypotheses drawn from the group 5 papers. Each is falsifiable and each is cheap to test with the harness from Deliverable 1.

H1, the layer axis. Motion information concentrates in the temporal modules at medium resolution rather than at the extremes. Xiao et al. find their motion feature works best after the first up-block and report that mid-resolution features give better motion correspondence than either shallow or deep ones. Prediction: motion selectivity peaks around up_blocks[1], not at the highest-resolution decoder layers and not at the bottleneck.

H2, the timestep axis. Motion is decided early, appearance late. This is a widely repeated piece of folklore that Baherwani et al. actually went and tested, finding two regimes with a reasonably consistent boundary between them. Prediction: motion selectivity is high in the first third of the denoising trajectory and falls off; content selectivity does the opposite. If the boundary sits somewhere different for AnimateDiff than they report, that is a result worth writing up, not a bug.

H3, the content confound. Raw activations, even in temporal layers, are dominated by appearance. Xiao et al. only get a clean motion signal after subtracting the per-frame mean across the frame axis, which removes the static component. Prediction: if we skip that normalisation, every layer will look content-selective and we will wrongly conclude there is no motion anywhere. This one matters most, because it is the mistake we would otherwise have made in week one of the sweep.

How H1 and H2 are actually decided

A prediction with no test statistic is not falsifiable in practice. For H1, the test is the argmax over blocks of the mean motion selectivity, with a 95% bootstrap confidence interval over pairs; H1 survives if that interval excludes both the shallowest and the deepest block. For H2, we fit a single changepoint over the timestep axis and report its location with a bootstrap interval; H2 survives if the changepoint falls in the first half of the trajectory. Both tests are written into the analysis script before the sweep runs, not chosen after seeing the map.

The measurement, and the distance function behind it

The previous draft defined the central figure in terms of "how far the activations move" without saying what distance that is. Since the whole project reduces to one heat map computed from that function, it is now specified. Let a be the cached activation at coordinate (L, t) for one clip, with a frame axis of length F = 16. Split it into a static and a dynamic part:

ā = meanF(a)   ·   ã = a − ā the static component, broadcast over frames, and the frame-mean-removed residual

For a pair of clips (A, B) the distance is one minus cosine similarity on the flattened tensor, taken on ā for the content channel and ã for the motion channel. Cosine rather than L2, because activation magnitudes differ by orders of magnitude between blocks and we need coordinates to be comparable; L2 is reported in an appendix as a robustness check, per Zhang and Nanda's advice that the metric should be fixed in advance and its alternatives shown.

This yields two maps, not one, which is the concrete form H3 takes:

Sdyn(L, t) = (dcã − dmã) / (dcã + dmã)      Sstat(L, t) = (dcā − dmā) / (dcā + dmā) dc is the distance across a content pair, dm across a motion pair; both maps land in [−1, 1]

The sign is what makes these useful. Positive means the coordinate responds to what is depicted, negative means it responds to how things move, near zero means it responds to both or to neither. Sdyn is the headline figure — motion isolated from the static component — and Sstat is printed beside it as the control that shows what H3 predicted: without the split, almost everything reads as content-selective. Publishing both is the difference between demonstrating the confound and merely asserting it.

Aggregation. The ratio is computed per pair and then averaged over pairs, never the other way round: a ratio of averages is not an average of ratios, and only the per-pair form supports a bootstrap interval. We report the median over pairs with a 95% bootstrap CI, within each seed, then average across the three seeds.

The correlational counterpart

Separately, fit a small linear probe on each coordinate's cached activation to predict the motion verb and, in a second probe, the subject family. Probe accuracy per coordinate says what information is present. The patching in Deliverable 3 says what information is used. These two can disagree, and where they disagree is the most interesting thing we are likely to find.

Protocol, because a probe on 120 clips overfits trivially. Logistic regression with L2 regularisation on the flattened, frame-mean-removed activation, reduced to 256 dimensions by PCA fitted on the training split only. The split is by scene: train on on a beach, test on in a forest, then swap and average. This forces the probe to find something about the verb rather than memorising a scene. The verb probe is 4-way (chance 25%) with 60 test clips per fold; the subject-family probe is binary (chance 50%). Chance level is plotted on every probe figure.

5.3 Deliverable 3: the patching framework

A patch replaces the activation at a chosen coordinate during clip B's generation with the one cached from clip A. Both runs share an initial noise tensor or the comparison is meaningless. Following Zhang and Nanda, our corruption is resampling — the replacement comes from a real second clip — and not Gaussian noising. That choice is stated because the two answer different questions and are routinely confused.

Four granularities, in increasing order of precision. We implement them in this order and stop when one works well enough:

  • 1. Whole tensor. Replace the full activation. Blunt, and likely to break coherence, but it establishes an upper bound on effect size.
  • 2. Channel subset. Replace only the most motion-selective channels. This needs a per-channel score, which S(L, t) does not provide because it aggregates over channels, so we define Sc(L, t, c) by the same formula restricted to channel c of the dynamic component and take the top k% by that score. k is swept over {1, 5, 10, 25} and reported as a curve (D-10). We expect this is where the method starts working.
  • 3. Attention weights only. Replace the temporal attention map rather than the features, in the spirit of MotionClone's observation that the dominant components of temporal attention are what actually drive motion.
  • 4. Scaled residual injection. Blend rather than replace, with a strength α from 0 to 1. Sweeping α over {0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1.0} gives a dose–response curve, which is far stronger evidence than a binary worked/did-not-work.

The patch window. A patch is applied over a contiguous range of timesteps, not a single one, and the range is a free parameter we sweep rather than a detail we leave unsaid: {one step}, {five steps centred on the peak}, {the first third}, {all 25}. Layer granularity is one module or one whole block. The winning (window, block) combination from the coarse pass is what Deliverable 4 uses.

Three controls, all of which we run before believing any positive result:

  • Null patch. Patch a clip with its own activation; the output must come back unchanged. In fp16 with cuDNN autotuning it will not be bit-identical, so the criterion is numerical rather than exact: mean absolute difference of the final latents below 1e-3 and PSNR above 45 dB between the decoded clips. Below that bar there is a bug in the harness and nothing downstream is trustworthy (D-11).
  • Random coordinate. Patch a coordinate with low |S|. The effect should be small. If a random layer does as well as our chosen one, we have not localised anything.
  • Symmetry. Patch A into B and B into A. The two effects should be approximate inverses. Asymmetry means something is leaking.

5.4 Deliverable 4: the demonstration, and how we score it

Two edits per test case, each with a clear target: keep A's motion while taking B's content, and the reverse. Twelve test pairs, both directions, so 24 edits — six from family Q, six from family H, spanning all four verbs, listed in the appendix of the report so the selection cannot be read as cherry-picked. Reported as a video grid plus numbers.

Content fidelity sc. CLIP similarity between output frames and the intended content prompt, plus CLIP or DINO image-to-image similarity against the reference clip's frames. The image-to-image version is the more honest of the two, since CLIP text similarity is easy to satisfy accidentally, and it is the one that enters the headline number.

Motion fidelity sm. A trajectory-based score in the style of Yatim et al.: extract point tracks from both clips with CoTracker and compare displacement vectors over time. Optical flow correlation via RAFT as a cheaper second opinion.

Video quality. Three VBench dimensions — subject consistency, motion smoothness, temporal flickering — so we can show the edit did not simply wreck the clip. Cheap to run, and it pre-empts the obvious objection.

The headline number

A single disentanglement score built from the four cross-similarities of one edit. For an output that should carry A's motion and B's content:

D = ½[ sc(out, B) + sm(out, A) ] − ½[ sc(out, A) + sm(out, B) ] sc is content similarity, sm is motion similarity, out is the edited clip

A correction the previous draft needed. As written, D adds a CLIP cosine (empirically ≈ 0.5 to 0.95) to a trajectory correlation (−1 to 1). Those are not commensurable and their sum is dominated by whichever has the wider spread. Both terms are therefore min–max normalised across the whole evaluation set before entering D, so D lands in [−1, 1] and the two axes contribute equally by construction. The raw un-normalised sc and sm are printed in the same table, so the normalisation hides nothing (D-14).

Positive and large means the edit moved the axis we aimed at and left the other one alone. Reported separately for both edit directions, because a method that works one way and not the other has not demonstrated disentanglement.

What would count as success, decided in advance

The previous draft never said what result would make us claim the project worked, which leaves the claim free to be fitted to whatever came out. Pre-registered, the claim is supported if all three hold:

  • median D over the twelve pairs is greater for patching than for the prompt-only baseline, and the 95% bootstrap CI of that difference excludes zero;
  • this holds in both edit directions, not just one;
  • VBench subject consistency for the edited clip is within 10% of the unedited source clip, so we are not buying the score by destroying the video.

If those are not met we report it as a negative result together with the α dose–response curve and the selectivity map, which remain findings in their own right. A localisation that does not survive intervention is exactly the disagreement between "present" and "used" that section 5.2 says is worth looking for.

Baselines. A disentanglement score with nothing to compare against is an unanchored number. We run: prompt-only (same seed, new prompt, no patching — mandatory, and if we cannot beat it we have built an expensive way to do nothing); MotionClone as the training-free reference, chosen over the MOFT controller so the comparison is decided rather than optional, on the grounds that its public code is closest in spirit to our granularity 3; and our own LoRA pair from 5.5 as the trained reference, which is cheaper and more directly comparable than running MotionDirector's full pipeline.

5.5 Deliverable 5: the trained model rewritten

The course requires a trained or finetuned model. Our method is training-free, so the previous draft satisfied the requirement with a one-paragraph promise of "a small MotionDirector-style pair of LoRAs, one or two days in Stage 6". That was the vaguest item in the plan and the easiest to arrive at unprepared. It is now a numbered deliverable with a recipe, a dataset, a budget, a feasibility pilot and — the important change — a job to do inside the project rather than beside it.

What we train

Two LoRA adapters on the same AnimateDiff pipeline, fitted separately, following MotionDirector's dual-path design.

  • Appearance LoRA — on the spatial path only: to_q / to_k / to_v / to_out.0 inside the transformer_blocks (both attn1 and attn2) of the frozen SD1.5 UNet. The key trick is appearance debiasing: train on a single randomly sampled frame per step, not the clip. A single frame contains no motion to learn, so the adapter is forced to absorb only identity, colour and texture.
  • Motion LoRA — the same projection names but filtered to modules whose path contains motion_modules. Trained on the full clip with the appearance LoRA attached and frozen, so the appearance component is already explained away and the temporal loss carries residual motion. Latents are residualised against an anchor frame before the temporal loss, as MotionDirector does.

In diffusers plus peft this is a LoraConfig whose target_modules is a regex, and the spatial/temporal split is just whether motion_modules appears in the module path — the same string filter the E3 hooks already use.

What we train it on

Four motion concepts drawn from the verb vocabulary already frozen in section 4.1 — running, walking, jumping, spinning — with roughly five real clips each from UCF101 and Kinetics-400, so about 20 clips in total. These are real videos, which is a deliberate departure from the probing set and is disclosed in the note on page 4 and in the report's data section. Training on our own generated clips instead was considered and rejected: it would make the LoRA a distillation of the model's existing behaviour and the comparison below would be circular.

The part that makes it earn its place

A generic LoRA pair would satisfy the checklist and teach us nothing. Instead we train the motion LoRA twice and compare, which turns a required artefact into a test of Deliverable 2:

  • Full-coverage motion LoRA — adapters on every temporal attention projection. This is the MotionDirector-equivalent and the trained baseline of section 5.4.
  • Probe-guided motion LoRA — adapters only on the top-k coordinates from Sdyn(L, t), which if H1 holds means up_blocks[1] and its neighbours. Same rank, same data, same steps, far fewer trainable parameters.

If the probe-guided variant matches the full-coverage one at a fraction of the parameters, our selectivity map has predictive power in weight space and not only in activation space, which is a considerably stronger claim than the patching result alone. If it does not match, that is a real negative result about the limits of the map, and a more interesting slide than "we also trained a LoRA". Either way it costs one extra training run, because the data, script and evaluation are already built.

A third view, nearly free. Take the trained full-coverage LoRAs and compute ‖ΔW‖ per module for the motion adapter against the appearance adapter. That gives a training-derived layer ranking to set against the two we already have: patching says what the model uses, the linear probe says what is present, and ‖ΔW‖ says where gradient descent chose to put the motion when asked. We report the rank correlation between all three. It is a dozen lines of code over weights we already have, and a three-way agreement (or disagreement) is the strongest single figure available to this project.

Feasibility and budget

E5 in Stage 1 records frames × resolution that fit, seconds per step and peak VRAM on a T4 — the training analogue of what E1 did for inference. The four training runs are then Stage 6 work at roughly 6 GPU-hours total; section 7 has the breakdown. We do not start LoRA training before the Stage 4 map exists, because the probe-guided variant depends on it.

5.6 Interface

A Gradio app. The important design decision is that it should expose the interpretability rather than hide it.

Inputs: two prompts (or two generated clips), a layer selector, a timestep range, and the patch strength α. Outputs: the edited clip, the three metric values, and the cross-attention heat map for the selected coordinate shown beside the selectivity map from Deliverable 2, so a user can see why that coordinate was chosen. A demo that only shows the output video would satisfy the requirement, but it would waste the project.

Precomputed, not live — a practical decision the previous draft left open. At 94 s per clip, a live generation inside a demo is a 94-second silence in a graded presentation. The app therefore serves a cache of the ~500 clips and all precomputed maps, and every combination reachable from the controls is one we have already generated. Live generation stays available behind an explicit button with a stated wait, for the one or two cases where an examiner asks for something off the grid. Confirm the expected scope with the TA regardless; the brief explicitly says to ask.

Appendix B. Risks, and what we do about them

Compute limits the layer × timestep sweep

Start coarse — one module per block, seven timesteps — and refine only around what looks promising. §7 shows the tiered plan fits in ≈ 36.5 GPU-hours where the naive version would have needed over 60.

AnimateDiff's split is too easy to be a finding

Fair criticism, and we say so up front. The answer is ModelScope T2V, where spatial and temporal were trained together. If the same structure shows up there, it generalises. The probe-guided LoRA in §5.5 is a second, cheaper answer to the same objection: it tests whether the map predicts anything, rather than whether the map merely reflects the architecture.

Everything looks content-selective and we find nothing

This is H3, and it is the failure mode we most expect. Subtract the per-frame mean before scoring, and publish Sstat beside Sdyn so the confound is demonstrated rather than asserted. Reading Xiao et al. saved us a week here.

Patched outputs come out incoherent rather than wrong

Run the null patch and the random-coordinate control before believing anything. Prefer scaled injection over hard replacement, and report the whole α curve. The VBench guard in the §5.4 success criterion exists so that an incoherent clip cannot score well.

The LoRA does not fit in 16 GB

E5 in Stage 1 is precisely this check, moved forward from Stage 6 for that reason. Fallback ladder: 8 frames at 256 × 256, then rank 8, then LoRA on the motion path only with the appearance path handled by prompt conditioning instead.

Stage 6 overruns

It is the densest week in the plan. Cut order is stated in §6: the full-coverage LoRA baseline goes first, the probe-guided LoRA is kept, because only the latter answers a question.

A decision gets quietly remade to suit the data

The §5.7 register is dated and append-only. Any change after 22 September carries a date and a reason.
